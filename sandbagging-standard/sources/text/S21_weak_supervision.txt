Removing Sandbagging in LLMs by Training with Weak Supervision
Emil Ryd 1 2 Henning Bartsch 1 Julian Stastny 3 Joe Benton * 4 Vivek Hebbar * 3
Abstract
As AI systems begin to automate complex tasks,
supervision increasingly relies on weaker models
or limited human oversight that cannot fully ver-
ify output quality. A model more capable than its
supervisors could exploit this gap through sand-
bagging, producing work that appears acceptable
but falls short of its true abilities. Can training
elicit a model’s best work even without reliable
verification? We study this using model organ-
isms trained to sandbag, testing elicitation tech-
niques on problem-solving math, graduate-level
science, and competitive coding tasks. We find
that training with weak supervision can reliably
elicit sandbagging models when supervised fine-
tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) are
combined: SFT on weak demonstrations breaks
the sandbagging behavior, enabling RL to then
fully elicit performance. Neither method succeeds
reliably alone—RL without SFT almost always
leads to reward hacking rather than genuine im-
provement, and SFT without RL fails to elicit full
performance when the supervisor is much weaker
than the untrusted model. Critically, this relies
on training being indistinguishable from deploy-
ment; when models can distinguish between train-
ing and deployment, they can perform well during
training while continuing to sandbag afterward.
Our results provide initial evidence that training
is a viable mitigation against sandbagging, while
highlighting the importance of making training
indistinguishable from deployment.

research, where we may have limited ability to verify the
quality of the AI’s work. Evaluating whether a research
direction is promising or whether an experiment is well-
designed is difficult, and we may have to rely on weaker
models or expensive (and limited) human oversight that can-

When we are concerned about a model sandbagging, a nat-
ural mitigation is to train the model on the relevant task to
elicit its capabilities. However, if we can only rely on the
supervision signal from weaker models that we trust, will
training still work? We study how well training works to
elicit performance from capable models that sandbag, when
the only supervision comes from weaker AI models. Can
training force a sandbagging model to produce useful work,
even when we cannot reliably verify its outputs?
We investigate this question as an adversarial game between
a red and a blue team, as in the AI control framework (Green-
blatt et al., 2024c). A blue team develops training protocols
to elicit useful work from the model, while a red team con-
structs attack policies to subvert those protocols. By giving
the red team knowledge of the blue team’s full training setup
and letting them optimize against it, we obtain conservative
estimates of how well training-based interventions work.
We evaluate supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforce-
ment learning (RL) (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al.,
2022; Bai et al., 2022) as elicitation techniques across three
settings: Olympiad math problems, Super GPQA science
questions, and Code Contests programming challenges. In
each setting, we train models using feedback from a weaker
supervisor and measure both the elicited performance of the
trained model and the rate at which it sandbags.
2. The Elicitation Game: Red vs. Blue Team
To study how training can elicit performance from sandbag-
ging models, we construct model organisms of sandbagging,
i.e. models deliberately trained to sandbag. If the sand-
bagging in our model organisms is more easily removed by
training than sandbagging in realistic misaligned models,
our results would be overly optimistic. So, to obtain conser-
vative estimates we formulate elicitation as an AI control
evaluation, with an adversarial game between a blue and a
red team (Greenblatt et al., 2024c). The red team constructs
model organisms designed to resist training, while the blue
team develops interventions to elicit performance. We play
both roles, analogous to designing a challenging benchmark
(red team) and evaluating methods on it (blue team).
2.1. AI Control Setup and Affordances
Let D denote the task distribution over problems that the
model will be asked to solve in deployment. We split the
task distribution into train and test splits, denoted as Dtrain
and Dtest respectively. Let U denote the strong untrusted
model andTdenote the weaker trusted model.
The AI control evaluation proceeds as follows: first, the blue
team commits to a training procedure, specifying the train-
ing distribution Dtrain, training data, and training method
(e.g. SFT and/or RL). The red team, with full knowledge
of this procedure, then constructs a model organism which
aims to survive training on Dtrain while performing poorly
on the deployment distribution Dtest. From the blue team’s
perspective, this ordering is a conservative assumption: the
red team knows what training will be applied and can opti-
mize against it.
2.1.1. RED TEAM AFFORDANCES
We give the red team the following affordances, intended to
allow us to upper bound the risk posed ifU were misaligned:
• TrainUbefore the blue team’s training begins.
• Specify a single system prompt (save for two flags,
below) used during both training and deployment.
• Set a binary flag in the system prompt indicating
whether to sandbag on the current problem. The red
team assigns this flag to each problem in advance (i.e.,
“sandbag” for 90% of problems, “don’t sandbag” for
10%), and the assignment remains fixed. This allows
the model to follow a conditional policy.
To avoid unrealistically aggressive sandbagging—which
would likely be detected easily in practice—we require that
the red team’s untrusted model roughly match the perfor-
mance of our weakest trusted model. Since our weakest
trusted model achieves approximately 10% on each of our
tasks, we constrain the red team to sandbag on at most 90%
of problems. The 90% sandbagging rate is higher than what
plausible threat models likely involve, and allowing such
a large sandbagging rate is a bias in favor of the red team,
which can sandbag more often. Nevertheless, we allow such
a large sandbagging rate to make it easier to measure the
effects of our interventions. We discuss potential improve-
ments to this setup in Section 8. All prompts are available
in Appendix B.2.
2.1.2. BLUE TEAM AFFORDANCES
The blue team must elicit the model that the red team pro-
duces, using the following affordances:
• Ability to train U on any data and reward signal they
can construct.
• Unlimited use of T on Dtrain. This includes (problem,
response) pairs that can be used for SFT, and ratings
of generated responses that can be used for RL.
• Limited ground truth (problem, correct response) pairs

2.1.3. EVALUATION
We measure two primary metrics:
• Sandbagging rate: The fraction of responses where
the model follows the red team’s instructions to pro-
duce a particular plausible-looking incorrect answer. A
sandbagging rate of 0% means the model never follows
the sandbagging instructions; 90% means it sandbags
whenever the system prompt flag is set.
• Elicited performance: The performance of the un-
trusted model on the setting.1
2.2. Model organisms of sandbagging
We consider three red team strategies to create model or-
ganisms with a conditional policy which underperforms
(sandbags) or sabotages at a pre-defined rate.
• Prompted: The red team creates a detailed sys-
tem prompt instructing U to sandbag by producing
plausible-looking incorrect responses. The prompt
contains a binary flag indicating whether to sandbag
on a given problem and—on Olympiads and Super
GPQA—a specific incorrect answer to output when
sandbagging. See Figure 1 for a schematic diagram.
• Prompt-distilled: The red team creates a detailed sys-
tem prompt instructing U to sandbag by producing
plausible-looking incorrect responses (same as for the
prompted model organism), then distills this into a
minimal prompt, containing only a binary flag indicat-
ing whether to sandbag on a given problem and—on
Olympiads and Super GPQA—a specific incorrect an-
swer to output when sandbagging.
1For evaluations, we let the blue team pick the best checkpoint

• Password-locked models: The red team password-
locks U, training it to imitate a weaker model when
the system prompt flag says so, and answer normally
otherwise (Roger & Greenblatt, 2023). During training
and deployment, the flag is set to make U perform like
the weak model in all cases.
We find that thepromptedmodel organism is the most
robust to training-based elicitation across all settings. Hence,
we report results on ourpromptedmodel organisms in the
main paper, and on ourprompt-distilledandpassword-
lockedmodel organisms in Appendix D. Before the red
team trains U, we perform RL training on the dataset to
ensure U is fully elicited. We require that the red team’s
model organism still attains U’s full benign performance
when setting the prompt flag to answer correctly, to ensure
that the red team has not removedU’s capabilities.
After red team training, we say the model issandbagging
when it follows its prompt to give the particular incorrect
answer provided. This means that we can have models that
still perform poorly without sandbagging—e.g. by giving a
different incorrect answer.
2.3. Setting
2.3.1. DATASETS
We use three task distributions which let us study training-
based control while having ground truth answers to measure:
• Olympiad math:Competition math problems from a
filtered version of NuminaMath (Li et al., 2024).
• Super GPQA:Graduate-level science questions that
require domain expertise (Du et al., 2025). Verifying
answers requires checking whether the reasoning steps
are logical and whether claims are factually accurate.
• Code Contests:Programming problems with gener-
ated test cases (Li et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025). To
make this setting harder for the blue team, we don’t
allow the blue team to run unit tests.
For Olympiads and Super GPQA, the red team instructs
the untrusted model to give a specific incorrect answer (e.g.
“42” or “A”).2 For Code Contests, the untrusted model is
instructed to insert a subtle bug, without being told which
specific bug to insert.

3. SFT on weak and strong samples
We first consider SFT as an elicitation technique for the blue
team. Given the prompted sandbagging model organism,
the blue team fine-tunes it on demonstrations from trusted
models of varying strength: thevery weaksupervisor, the
weaksupervisor, thestrongsupervisor, and strongground
truthsamples (generated by a stronger model and filtered
for correctness).
Weak supervisor demonstrations are off-policy relative to
sandbagging: they lack the specific features of a sandbag-
ging response (e.g., the particular wrong answer the red
team’s prompt requests). Training on them may therefore
push the untrusted model away from following its sand-
bagging instructions, even without teaching it to be correct.
However, prolonged SFT on weak samples will eventually
degrade capabilities, so successful elicitation requires break-
ing the sandbagging policy before degradation occurs.
In Figure 2, we show elicited performance and sandbagging
rate across all three settings for SFT runs on datasets of
various sample counts.
SFT on weak samples elicits nearly full performance.
Across settings, fine-tuning onweaksupervisor samples
reduces the sandbagging rate to near-zero and elicits sub-
stantial benign performance. Elicited performance often
exceeds the weak supervisor’s own performance on the
task, but does not reach the full benign performance of
the untrusted model. This is consistent with findings from
prior work on password-locked models (Greenblatt et al.,
2024b). With SFT onweaksupervisor samples (4o-mini),

5. SFT to reduce sandbagging, RL to elicit
As found in Sections 3 and 4, RL fails to elicit the untrusted
model due to low exploration when it consistently follows
its sandbagging policy, and SFT alone fails to fully elicit
benign capabilities with onlyvery weaksupervision. To
combat the individual shortcomings of these two techniques,
a natural strategy is to first fine-tuneU on a small number of
samples from the weak supervisor, followed by RL training.
To pick a consistent strategy, we fine-tune for 80 steps when
using samples from theweaksupervisor, and 100 steps from
thevery weaksupervisor. The step counts were chosen to
be the smallest number of steps that reduces sandbagging
enough for RL to start working, to avoid overfitting to the
weak supervisor’s rollouts. We observe limited variance
between different RL runs after this initial SFT init (see
Appendix D.10), and average over two seeds per run.

6. Red team uplifts
Since the blue team manages to almost fully elicit the red
team’s model organisms (see Table 1) across our three set-
tings using bothweakandvery weaksupervision, we study
how various increased affordances for the red team may
change the outcomes. We consider two uplifts for the
red team: being aware when the untrusted model is being
trained, and starting with a reward hacking model organ-
ism. In Appendix D.8, we also consider attack selection,
i.e. sandbagging only on a subdistribution where the trusted
supervisor is worse at supervising.
6.1. Situationally aware during training
First, we investigate a scenario where the elicitation train-
ing is no longer indistinguishable from deployment. In this
case, the red team can subvert the training by preventing
generalization from the training to the deployment distri-
bution (Greenblatt et al., 2024a). We keep the training and
deployment distributions i.i.d., but give the red team perfect
knowledge of which samples are in the training distribution
and which are in the deployment distribution.
Given this perfect knowledge about training and deploy-
ment samples, the red team instructs the untrusted model to
always answer benignly on the training distribution and to
sandbag only on the deployment distribution by setting the
flag in the system prompt. This means that the blue team’s
elicitation training now does not train the untrusted model
to directly disobey its malign system prompt, meaning that
it may still follow the sandbagging instructions during de-
ployment (Wichers et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2025).
We show the results of SFT followed by RL in this setup
in Figure 4. Across all settings, the untrusted model gets
less elicited than in the base case (for thevery weaksuper-
visor: 36% vs 88% on Olympiads, 60% vs 99% on Super
GPQA, and 66% vs 93% on Code Contests). On multiple
runs (for instance on thevery weaksupervisor on Super
GPQA and Olympiads) we observe that the sandbagging
rate even increases (and elicited performance decreases) dur-
ing RL training after having been partially removed during
SFT. Given these results, we tentatively conclude that mak-
ing sure training is indistinguishable from deployment is
essential to ensure successful elicitation of an adversarially
sandbagging model.

7. Related Work
Sandbaggingrefers to strategic underperformance where
models deliberately perform below their true capabilities
(van der Weij et al., 2024). Prior work has studied both
detecting sandbagging (Tice et al., 2024; Taylor et al., 2025)
and training it away through capability elicitation (Green-
blatt et al., 2024b; Benton et al., 2024). The most similar
work is Greenblatt et al. (2024b), which creates password-
locked model organisms that conditionally sandbag. A key
difference is that we also study models actively trying to
subvert training, not merely imitating weaker performance.
AI controlstudies how to safely deploy AI systems under
the assumption they may be misaligned or attempting to sab-
otage oversight (Greenblatt et al., 2024c; Carlsmith, 2023).
We adopt the red team versus blue team framework from
prior work (Greenblatt et al., 2024c; Griffin et al., 2024;
Bhatt et al., 2025). Previous control research has focused on
monitoring-based techniques (Greenblatt et al., 2024c; Ben-
ton et al., 2024; Korbak et al., 2025); our work complements
this by studying training as a control technique.
Scalable oversightstudies how weaker supervisors can ef-
fectively oversee stronger AI models (Leike et al., 2018;
Bowman et al., 2022; Burns et al., 2023). Our work stud-
ies (simple forms of) scalable oversight under adversarial
assumptions: the strong model is actively trying to evade
weak oversight. If weak supervision succeeds even against
models trying to subvert it, this provides stronger safety
guarantees than evaluations assuming cooperative models.
8. Discussion
8.1. How representative are our results?
We study training-based elicitation because we are con-
cerned about misaligned AI systems sandbagging on impor-
tant real-world tasks, such as alignment research. Because
these tasks are difficult to study directly, we use simpler
settings as a proxy. In these settings, the blue team wins de-
cisively even with only weak supervision. How informative
should we expect these results to be about the settings we
care about?
The primary reason to doubt that our results will transfer is
that our model organisms may be unrepresentative of actu-
ally misaligned AIs; we discuss this further in Section 8.2.
Beyond this, we think the following are the most important
reasons why our results might not transfer to more realistic
settings.
• In complex, multi-step tasks such as agentic coding
or research, a misaligned model has a much larger
action space and more opportunities for subtle sabo-
tage. A single well-targeted action—such as reading
from an incorrect dataset file or introducing a hard-
to-detect logical error—could compromise an entire
project while remaining difficult to catch (Gasteiger
et al., 2025). This would favor the red team.
• Training-based elicitation may be less sample efficient
on longer, more complex tasks, requiring substantially
8

more training data or compute to break a sandbagging
policy. This would favor the red team.
• In more complex settings, the blue team gains access
to more granular supervision strategies. Process-based
supervision (Uesato et al., 2022; Lightman et al., 2023)
would allow the blue team to monitor individual actions
rather than evaluating only final outputs, potentially
catching subtle sabotage that outcome-based evaluation
would miss. This would favor the blue team.
• Our setup is conservative in that the red team has full
knowledge of the blue team’s training procedure and
can optimize against it. In practice, a misaligned model
is unlikely to have such precise knowledge. This would
favor the blue team.
• For some tasks, weaker models may have close to no
useful supervision ability, which would favor the red
team.
In conclusion, we think our results provide some evidence
that training with weak supervision will be a viable approach
for eliciting misaligned AIs that are sandbagging on criti-
cal tasks, but we would be substantially more confident if
we had results on model organisms we thought were more
representative of actually misaligned AIs.
8.2. Limitations
Narrow model organisms.Our red team’s attack policy
is created through narrow supervised fine-tuning and RL,
which may instill the malign policy more shallowly than
would occur in a naturally emerging scheming model (Carl-
smith, 2023). A model that has developed scheming ten-
dencies through pretraining or extensive RL may be more
resistant to blue team training (Hubinger et al., 2024). In
Appendix D, we stress-test our model organisms, and find
results that indicate that it’s possible to at least partially
elicit our model organisms in ways that might not transfer
to more realistic model organisms.
Two outcomes vs. three outcomes.Our settings do not
distinguish between benign failures and attacks. A more re-
alistic three-outcome setting likely favors the red team: even
if the blue team elicits nearly 100% of benign performance,
the red team might win by maintaining a small targeted
sabotage rate on critical samples. Future work should inves-
tigate whether our results hold when sabotage and honest
failure are distinguished.
9. Conclusion
We present the first empirical study of training as a control
technique, investigating whether SFT and RL can elicit a
sandbagging model’s true capabilities when supervision
comes only from weaker models.
Our key findings:
1. SFT alone does not reliably elicit near-full perfor-
mance without ground truth access.Fine-tuning on
just 10ground truthsamples elicits 83–99% of benign
performance across settings, or almost full elicitation.
SFT on weaker demonstrations drives the sandbagging
rate to near zero in both theweakandvery weakcase,
but onlyweaksupervisor demonstrations elicit near-
full performance (85–94%); withvery weaksupervi-
sor demonstrations, the untrusted model’s capabilities
degrade during training, and SFT only partially elicits
benign performance (40–75%).
2. RL alone with weak supervision is unreliable
against consistent sandbaggers.When the untrusted
model follows a consistent sandbagging policy, RL
against theweakorvery weaksupervisor sometimes
leads to elicitation, but often results in reward hacking.
This occurs even when the sandbagging model acts
benignly on a substantial fraction of samples.
3. SFT followed by RL using weaker supervisors suc-
ceeds.The blue team wins convincingly across all
settings by first using SFT on weaker supervisor sam-
ples to reduce the sandbagging rate, then applying RL
against a weaker supervisor to elicit remaining perfor-
mance. Hence, the blue team succeeds at eliciting the
sandbagging model even without access to anyground
truthfor training.
4. Off-distribution RL favors the red team.When train-
ing and deployment distributions differ, the sandbag-
ging model can exploit this gap by completing tasks
correctly during training and sandbagging only dur-
ing deployment, substantially reducing the amount of
elicitation by both SFT and RL. This highlights the im-
portance of on-distribution training for training-based
elicitation to succeed.

A. Setting details
A.1. Dataset filtering
We construct versions of our three datasets, Olympiads, Super GPQA, & Code Contests that enhance the difference in
performance between U and T by first filtering the datasets in the following way: we take a model stronger than U, Claude
4.5 Sonnet for Olympiads & Super GPQA, and Claude 4.5 Opus for Code Contests, and filter away all questions that the
stronger model gets wrong (pass@1). We find that this increases the performance gap betweenU and T by 10-15 percentage
points. We use these filtered datasets for all experiments. We split our datasets into an ”red team” split (20%), used for first
eliciting the untrusted model fully and for the red team to train their malign initialization, and a ”blue team” (80%), used by
the blue team to elicit the red team’s malign elicitation.
A.2. Generating incorrect answers
To generate incorrect answers to give in the system prompt for the red team, we let the red team prompt the untrusted model4
to first generate a correct solution to each problem, and then come up with its most plausible incorrect answer. We then
store these for each setting and use in our experiments. Since these ”incorrect answers” were generated by U (which does
not perfectly know the answer to every question), they can in fact be correct. We believe this is fair, since this dynamic
would still exist for a powerful misaligned model trying to sandbag on a task. On open-ended questions, like in Olympiads,
the rate of proposed incorrect answers that are correct is actually very small, roughly 2%. On Super GPQA, however,
which is multiple-choice, the rate is substantially higher, around 10%. This is why the ground truth performance even when
sandbagging in 100% of cases is non-zero, and why the sandbagging rate does not go all the way to zero even when models
are fully elicited during training.

A.3. Benign elicitation
Before the red team trains U to construct a malign initialization, we train U via reinforcement learning with ground truth
reward on the ”red team” split. We find that this leads to an extra 3% of performance on Olympiads, 10% extra performance
on Code Contests, and no significant performance improvement on Super GPQA. We believe the elicitation on Code Contests
& Olympiads occurs because the untrusted model learns how to format its code to pass the test cases, rather than learning
new capabilities.

B.1.1. PROMPT DISTILLATION

pick the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss.

B.3. Password-locked model organism
We train password-locked models using the same methodology as Greenblatt et al. (2024b). We generate 5 rollouts for
each sample in our ”red team” split (see A.1) from U, as ourstrongmodel, and 5 rollouts for each sample from Ministral
3B (Liu et al., 2026), as ourweakmodel. We choose this model as it is roughly at the level of ourvery weaksupervisor,
Llama 8b. We filter the samples from thestrongmodel to only retain correct answers. We then fine-tune U on the
complete dataset of rollouts from theweakandstrongmodel, with a flag in the system prompt indicating whether the model
should reply as the strong or the weak model. We use the same flags as for the prompt distilled model organisms, with
“ANSWER INCORRECTLY” locking to answers from Ministral 3B, and “ANSWER CORRECTLY” locking to answers
from gpt-oss-20b. We fine-tune with a batch size of 32 for 5 epochs (roughly 1000 steps), and again, pick the checkpoint
with the lowest validation loss.
C. Blue team training details
C.1. Supervised fine-tuning
We generate samples from the weak supervisors by prompting them with a benign system prompt. For each trusted
supervisor, we construct datasets consisting of 1, 4, 10, 100, & 1000 samples. We construct validation datasets of size
max(0.1∗D,1) , where D is the size of the training dataset. We fine-tune without reasoning trace, and mask loss on
the reasoning trace tokens. We find that without this masking, the untrusted (reasoning) model loses its ability to output
reasoning traces. We use a batch size ofmin(D,32)for the different subsets, and use a learning rate of1e−4.
To decide a learning rate, we performed a hyperparameter sweep over learning rates [1e−5,3e−5,5e−5,1e−4,3e−4]
on Olympiads and Super GPQA (see Figure 6 for results from the LR sweep on Olympiads) and concluded that 1e−4 was
the best learning rate overall, although both3e−5and5e−5were comparable.
Note:For the SFT+RL experiments in Section 5 we actually use a learning rate of 3e−5 for the SFT part of the training
run. This is simply because we previously used this learning rate during SFT, before settling on 1e−4 as our primary SFT
learning rate. Because SFT+RL already works with this learning rate, we did not rerun it with a higher learning rate.

C.2.1. TRAINING DETAILS

D.4. Stress-testing the sandbagging model organisms
To study the robustness of our model organisms, we try some very simple training methods that we wouldn’t expect to work
against capable sandbagging models (using both SFT and RL) and see whether they are enough to elicit our sandbagging
model organisms. We find that all our model organisms are susceptible to at least some elicitation from many simple SFT
methods. Our prompted MOs are stronger than our prompt-distilled MOs, who get elicited even by RL on completely
random rewards.
D.4.1. TRIVIALSFTMETHODS
We try three trivial SFT baseline methods by (Prasanna et al., 2026) on our prompted model organisms: off-distribution
SFT, off-distribution pirate SFT, & off-distribution anti-sandbagging SFT (we explain what these entail below). If they
successfully elicit near full performance on our model organisms, this would cast doubt on the robustness of our model
organisms and the claimed superiority of our strongest technique, SFT followed by RL. Below we explain how each method
works:
•Off-distribution SFT: SFT onstronglabels on a different task distribution than we’re evaling on.
•Off-distributionpirateSFT: Off-distribution SFT, but the model completions are written in pirate-speak.
• Off-distributionanti-sandbaggingSFT: Off-distribution SFT, but the model is instructed to sandbag during SFT
training.
We call these methods trivial, because they don’t require any samples on the actual evaling distribution. That said, the
task distributions we train on arerelativelysimilar to the ones we evaluate on, which makes these methods slightly more
non-trivial.
We report the results for off-distribution SFT in Figure 15, off-distributionpirateSFT in Figure 16, and off-distribution
anti-sandbaggingSFT in Figure 17.

We find that all of these trivial baseline methods elicit at least some performance, and the strongest,anti-sandbagging SFT,

of the two runs on Olympiads. Prasanna et al. (in forthcoming work) have shown that training on random rewards can lead
to substantial performance improvements on real settings, e.g. math. They argue that the reason for non-trivial gradient
updates from RL on random rewards comes from a bias induced by the clipping in the GRPO reward function. We, however,
do not have any clipping in our RL runs, and still see elicitation.
We suspect that in our case, RL on random rewards is akin to adding noise to the model weights, and that because of the
brittleness of the prompt-distilled MO, this noising forces the model back to its original, non-sandbagging policy. The fact
that RL on random reward works on the prompt-distilled model organism is evidence that it is not a very robust model
organism. For this reason, we think that results on this model organism—reported in Appendix D.1—should be taken with a
pinch of salt. We also recommend performing this baseline experiment on future model organisms.
As can be seen in Figure 18, the prompted model organism is not elicited by RL on random rewards on any run across all
settings. We interpret this as further evidence that the prompted model organism is more robust than the prompt-distilled,
and hence we use it as the main model organism of this paper.

D.6. Effects of exploration on reward hacking
D.6.1. THE IMPORTANCE OF DOING THE RIGHT AMOUNT OFSFT
The fact that the untrusted model manages to reward hack the weaker supervisors when RL is applied directly (Section 4)
but not after an initial SFT stage implies that there should be aphase shiftoccurring in terms of the final RL outcome as you
scale up the amount of SFT from 0, from reward hacking to elicitation. With no SFT, the sandbagging rate at the start of
RL is high, and the untrusted model learns to reward hack. After enough SFT, the sandbagging rate is low enough and the
model learns through RL to produce genuinely correct answers.
In an experiment done on a previous model organism, which was prompted instead of prompt-distilled, and based on
gpt-oss-20b, not 120b, we investigated this dynamic by varying the amount of initial SFT before applying RL. Using thevery
weaksupervisor on Super GPQA, we show in Figure 19 that models with high initial sandbagging rates achieve similar final
rewards to those with low sandbagging rates, but substantially lower ground truth accuracy. This reveals a phase transition:
below a critical sandbagging rate threshold, RL elicits genuine performance; above it, RL leads to reward hacking.
D.6.2. REWARD HACKING INDUCED BY LOW EXPLORATION
We mention in Section 4 that we believe the primary reason that doing SFT before RL is beneficial for the blue team is that
it increases the exploration of the untrusted model at the start of RL training. However, our experiments in the main paper
do not let us distinguish between this hypothesis and the hypothesis that SFT helps because it reduces the untrusted model’s
propensity for and/or ability to reward hack on a given sample.
To test this further, we run an experiment to isolate the exploration. We run the red team’s RL training, which teaches the
untrusted model to follow its conditional sandbagging policy, increasing the conditional sandbagging rate from 99% to
99.9%. However, we remove the part of the red team’s training reward which encourages the model to also give convincing
answers. We then start blue team RL from various checkpoints along the red team’s training. Later checkpoints (after more
RL steps) should thus have lower exploration of correct answers, but not be any more convincing to the trusted model. We
find (see Figure 20) that elicitation happens only when training from the first checkpoints – all other runs result in reward
hacking. We interpret this as evidence that low exploration can induce reward hacking against a weak supervisor.

D.7. Effect of sandbagging rate on elicitation
In the main paper, we let the red team sandbag on up to 90% of all samples. We think this could be a reasonable affordance
for a model that sandbags on fuzzy tasks where the blue team’s ability to supervise is extremely weak, e.g. conceptual
alignment research. However, for more verifiable tasks, such as the ones we study, such a high sandbagging rate is likely
unrealistic, and would easily be caught by the blue team. Nevertheless, we choose to allow the red team such a high
sandbagging rate to increase the effect sizes of our experiments, relying on the fact that the absolute sandbagging rate might
not matter for whether sandbagging works within a large range of values.
In this experiment, we study whether this assumption is true, or if the absolute sandbagging rate has an effect on elicitation
rates. We vary the sandbagging rate from 10 to 90% while training withweaksupervision on Super GPQA and Olympiads.
Results are shown in Figure 21.
As can be seen in Figure 21, on both Olympiads and Super GPQA, varying the attack rate did not cause any differences in the
(in)effectiveness of RL with weak supervision. This is not an exhaustive experiment, but indicates that lower sandbagging
rates do not meaningfully weaken the sandbagging MO’s robustness against elicitation.
D.8. Attack selection as a red team uplift
In our default setup, the red team chooses samples at random on which to sandbag. However, a more promising strategy is
to only sandbag on more difficult questions, that the trusted model might struggle to supervise. We refer to this asattack
selection. We operationalize this by giving the red team perfect knowledge of which questions the trusted supervisor could
and couldn’t solve. The red team then sets their system prompt flag such that the untrusted model sandbags only on samples
that the weaker, trusted supervisor got wrong. We find that the trusted supervisors have worse discriminating ability between
correct and incorrect answers on questions they can’t answer, compared to questions they can answer.
Figure 22 shows that, on Olympiads and Code Contests, we find no difference in outcomes between the attack selection and
default model organism. On Super GPQA, the attack selection model organism of the red team outperforms the default
model organism when training against theweaksupervisor. It retains a high sandbagging rate throughout RL training, but
gets elicited when trained against thevery weaksupervisor.
We generally find that attack selection does not help the red team, except for the singular case of training on thevery weak
supervisor on Super GPQA. That said, we suspect that clever attack selection is a potentially effective strategy for a capable,
misaligned AI to employ against weaker supervisors.
