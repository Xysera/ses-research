Abstract
With increasing autonomy, Large Language Models (LLMs) may engage in de-
ceptive behaviors, such as scheming to pursue misaligned goals or sandbagging
to escape oversight. Monitoring outputs alone is insufficient, as models can pro-
duce seemingly benign responses while harboring misaligned internal objectives.
While mechanistic approaches like linear probing are promising, they rely on the
assumption that deception corresponds to a single direction in representation space.
We demonstrate that such single-direction probes fail to generalize across diverse
deception scenarios. To bridge this gap, we propose representing deception as a
multi-dimensional subspace rather than a single direction. Our method utilizes
iterative orthogonal decomposition to identify complementary directions that cap-
ture distinct facets of deceptive intent. To effectively leverage these directions
for detection, we find that static combinations prove brittle due to strong domain
dependence across different scenarios. We therefore introduce a domain-adaptive
gating mechanism that dynamically composes the extracted directions through
few-shot adaptation at test time. With only 10 examples, our approach achieves an
average AUROC of 0.97 and 94% recall at 5% FPR across all evaluated datasets,
substantially outperforming existing methods.
1 Introduction
As LLMs are granted increasing autonomy in high-stakes domains, concerns have grown regard-
ing their potential for deceptive behavior [ 5, 6, 14, 16, 27]. Deception refers to cases in which a
model strategically produces misleading signals to advance objectives that are misaligned with the
intentions of its developers or users. Such behaviors can take multiple forms, including in-context
scheming [19], where hidden goals are pursued during deployment; sandbagging [3, 16], in which
performance is intentionally degraded during evaluation to evade oversight; and the concealment
of long-term objectives to avoid modification during training [ 8, 11, 21, 25]. These risks render
deception a distinct safety challenge requiring targeted countermeasures. However, training-based
mitigation approaches [24], such as enforcing alignment objectives through reinforcement learn-
ing, may introduce new failure modes, including reward hacking and superficially compliant yet
strategically misaligned behaviors [2, 13]. In contrast, detection-based methods [10, 12] provide a
complementary and lightweight line of defense that does not require modifying the model, enabling
monitoring, timely intervention when preventive measures fail.
Most existing detection approaches rely on analyzing model outputs [12, 17]. However, this strategy
is limited because a sufficiently capable model can produce benign-looking responses while internally
representing misaligned goals, masking deceptive intent. This limitation has motivated a shift toward
mechanistic interpretability methods [18, 29] that probe models’ internal representations rather than

surface behavior alone. A prominent approach is linear probing [10], where classifiers are trained on
intermediate activations to identify directions in the representation space associated with deceptive
behavior. In principle, this enables detection even when outputs appear compliant.
Nevertheless, representation-based detection methods often rely on a strong and under-examined
assumption that deception corresponds to a single, universal direction in the representation
space [ 10, 29]. This assumption treats deception as a monolithic construct with consistent in-
ternal signatures across tasks and contexts, potentially overlooking the diverse and domain-dependent
nature of deceptive strategies in advanced LLMs. As demonstrated in Table 1, such single-direction
formulations generalize poorly across datasets and suffer substantial performance degradation when
applied to heterogeneous deceptive scenarios, indicating that internal representations of deception are
not universally aligned across domains.
Motivated by this phenomenon, we aim to design a robust detector capable of identifying deceptive
behaviors across diverse datasets and their corresponding domains. Rather than modeling deception
with a single vector, we represent it as a multi-dimensional subspace in which different directions
capture complementary deception-related features. This formulation naturally raises two challenges:
first, how to identify multiple separable directions that meaningfully characterize distinct aspects of
deception; and second, how to combine these directions effectively, given that deceptive behaviors
vary substantially across datasets and domains. Static combination strategies, such as simple adding,
are insufficient in this setting, as directions that are informative in one domain may be irrelevant or
noisy in another, motivating the need for adaptive composition mechanisms.
To address this, we proposeDynamic Deception Subspace Navigation (DDSN), a novel method
that dynamically navigates the deception subspace to precisely capture LLM deception under varying
dataset conditions. First, we use Iterative Orthogonal Subspace Decomposition (IOSD) to extract a set
of complementary directions, ensuring that each subsequent component captures residual deceptive
information orthogonal to previous ones. Second, to handle the challenge of domain variability, we
propose a Domain-Adaptive Gating Mechanism. Rather than relying on fixed weights, this mechanism
leverages test-time few-shot adaptation to dynamically combine the extracted directions based on the
specific characteristics of the dataset. This enables the model to focus on the most relevant deceptive
features for the given domain. Experimentally, our method demonstrates exceptional efficiency and
robustness: with just 10 adaptation examples, it achieves an average AUROC of 0.97 and a recall
of 95% at a 5% FPR across evaluated datasets, significantly outperforming existing static probing
methods. The 10-sample requirement is reasonable in practice: the detector transfers across related
domains without re-adaptation (Figure 3), so new examples are only needed when a substantial
context shift occurs, which can be flagged by a lightweight off-the-shelf classifier.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
➊ We reveal that deception in LLMs manifests differently across various datasets, challenging
the prevailing assumption of a universal, single-direction detector. Our empirical analysis
demonstrates that single-direction probes fail to generalize, highlighting the need for a more
nuanced approach to deception detection.
➋ We introduce Dynamic Deception Subspace Navigation (DDSN), a novel method that com-
bines Iterative Orthogonal Subspace Decomposition with an Adaptive Gating Network. This
approach effectively disentangles complementary deceptive features and dynamically adjusts
their importance based on dataset characteristics, addressing the limitations of static detection.
➌ Extensive evaluations across multiple diverse datasets demonstrate that our method significantly
outperforms existing baselines. DDSN achieves superior detection accuracy and generalization
with minimal adaptation data, requiring only 10 examples per dataset.

2.2 Detection for LLM Deception
Prior works have explored approaches to detect deception in LLMs, categorized into black-box
and white-box methods. Black-box approaches primarily focus on model outputs. [20] develops a
classifier using responses to multiple-choice follow-up questions. [12, 17] utilize chain-of-thought
monitoring. While these methods offer valuable insights, they may not fully capture the complex
nature of deceptive behaviors in LLMs, especially when models maintain a facade of appropriate
responses despite internal misalignment. This challenge underscores the importance of white-box
techniques that can directly examine the model’s internal representations. Addressing this, [4] propose
an innovative unsupervised approach that identifies directions in the embedding space associated
with logical consistency across negated statements. Complementing this, [18] explore the efficacy of
straightforward statistical methods, demonstrating that even simple difference-in-means probes can
yield meaningful results in detecting deceptive patterns. Recent work by [10] advances this field by
introducing linear probes that monitor model activations, evaluating their effectiveness in realistic
scenarios of deceptive behavior. Despite these advancements, the generalizability of these methods
remains limited, often relying on the assumption of a universal direction effective across all datasets.

5 Experiments
5.1 Experimental Settings
Models.For fair comparison with prior methods and because deceptive behavior in frontier-scale
models is of greater concern, we follow [10] and useLlama-3.3-70B-Instruct[ 9] as our main model.
As a frontier-scale, open-source model, it represents a realistic and high-impact setting in which
deceptive capabilities are both plausible and safety-critical. We additionally includeQwen-3-32B[28]
to evaluate effectiveness across different model families.
Datasets.We divide our data into three functional categories:Source Domainsfor subspace extraction,
Target Domainsfor adaptation and evaluation, and aControl Domainfor threshold calibration.
Source Domains (Training).We extract the initial deception subspace using two datasets that serve
as the basis for IOSD: (1)Instructed Pairs[ 29]: Unlike standard behavioral datasets, this uses an
off-policy approach. Each sample contains a contrastive instruction (prompting honesty or deception)
followed by a ground-truth fact [1]. We use activations from the fact tokens (truncating the last 5) to
capture the internal preparation for deception before the generation of falsehoods. (2)Roleplaying
Scenarios[ 10]: Simple roleplay setups where the model adopts a deceptive persona. These sources
provide the primary basis vectors for our subspace.
Target Domains (Evaluation & Adaptation).We evaluate our method on a diverse set of datasets
covering different types of complex deceptive behaviors. For Domain-Adaptive Gating, we use 10
randomly selected examples from each dataset for few-shot adaptation and reserve the remaining
samples for evaluation.
• Strategic Scheming:We useInsider Tradingdataset [ 23] (Report and Confirmation
subsets), where models conceal illegal trading. We also includeSandbagging[ 3], where
models strategically underperform to evade evaluation.
• Instrumental Deception:We useAI Liar[ 20]. Here, agents lie to maximize rewards (e.g.,
a doctor prescribing inferior treatment) without explicit "Yes/No" forced choices.
• Context-Conflicted Honesty:Using theMASKdataest [ 22], we could test scenarios where
agents are given ground-truth facts but are pressured by user prompts to contradict them.

Baselines.We compare our method against a diverse set of baselines, including supervised linear

we follow [10] and train a static logistic regression probe on raw activations from theInstructed
Pairsdataset, which the original paper identifies as the supervision source yielding the strongest
generalization performance among available alternatives. To ensure a fair comparison with adaptive
baselines, we additionally evaluate a few-shot variant of the linear probe that incorporates the same
target-domain examples used by few-shot black-box monitoring. We further include two mechanistic
baselines based on activation statistics: Difference of Means and Linear Discriminant Analysis
(LDA) [18]. Finally, we evaluate GPT-4o-based black-box monitoring as an external judge operating
solely on behavioral outputs, considering both a standard prompt-based setting and a few-shot variant
augmented with target-domain examples.
Evaluation Metrics.We report three metrics to evaluate both discriminative power and reliability.
First, we use the AUROC to measure separability between deceptive and honest representations
in a threshold-independent manner. To reflect deployment scenarios where false alarms must be
tightly controlled, we additionally report deceptive recall at a 5% false positive rate on benign control
samples (Recall@5%FPR), which quantifies the fraction of deceptive examples detected under a
strict error constraint. Finally, to assess generalization beyond the control distribution, we report
the false positive rate on the target honest samples at the same calibrated threshold (FPR@5%FPR),
measuring how often honest behaviors are misclassified when the detector maintains a 5% FPR.
5.2 Main Results
Comparison with Baselines.Across datasets with diverse and complex deceptive behaviors (Table 1,
Figure 2), baseline methods exhibit clear limitations. Linear probing generalizes poorly to benchmarks
with heterogeneous deception patterns (e.g., Mask, PKU-DeceptionBench, THU-DeceptionBench)
and often attains high deceptive recall only at the cost of extremely high false positive rates on
honest samples. Adding few-shot supervision yields only marginal improvements and does not
resolve this trade-off. Difference of Means and LDA occasionally outperform linear probes, but still
suffer from elevated false positives, indicating that a single global direction is insufficient to capture
deception across diverse contexts. Black-box monitoring reduces false positives, yet degrades on
implicit or compositional deception (e.g., PKU-DeceptionBench), where detection remains limited
even with few-shot prompting. In contrast, our method consistently achieves strong and balanced
performance across all datasets and model families, reaching average AUROC of 0.97 on Llama-3.3-
70B-Instruct and 0.93 on Qwen3-32B, while maintaining high recall with acceptable false positive
rates. Although Qwen3-32B shows increased confusion on transaction-related insider scenarios due
to partial representation overlap, our approach still surpasses all baselines by a substantial margin.

gating token-level anomaly scores into the detection score, including simple averaging, maximum
pooling, and sliding-window aggregation with different window sizes and strides. We find that max-
based aggregation consistently underperforms mean aggregation in terms of AUROC and deceptive
recall, indicating that relying on the single highest token-level score is insufficient to characterize
sequence-level deception. Sliding-window methods partially mitigate this issue by requiring localized
consistency within a window, but their performance remains sensitive to window size and stride, and
they generally trade off recall for lower FPR. In contrast, mean aggregation achieves the strongest
overall separability and the highest deceptive recall while maintaining comparable false positive rates,
suggesting that deception in most cases is better reflected as a distributed signal across the sequence
rather than isolated spikes. Based on this consistent advantage and its simplicity, we adopt mean
aggregation as the default scoring strategy in all experiments.

related features than a single direction and therefore transfers more reliably across settings. As
shown in Figure 3, we adapt the method using examples from one dataset and directly evaluate it
on three distinct benchmarks, including AI Liar, PKU-DeceptionBench, and THU-DeceptionBench.

5.3 Gating Adaptation Analysis
As illustrated in Figure 4, we extract seven orthogonal directions from LLaMA-3.3-70B-Instruct with
IOSD. Direction 1 consistently receives the primary gating weight across datasets, which is reasonable
since the first extracted direction captures the most prominent and globally shared component of the
learned representation. However, its activation magnitude still exhibits substantial variation across
datasets, indicating that even this dominant direction is modulated in a domain-dependent manner.
Moreover, the remaining directions show dataset-specific gating patterns, with different domains
assigning higher importance to different auxiliary components. These observations demonstrate
that cross-domain adaptation cannot be adequately characterized by a single universal direction, but
instead relies on the dynamic reweighting of multiple sub-components within the learned subspace.
6 Discussion
Our results highlight several advantages of domain-adaptive subspace navigation over static deception
detection. As illustrated in Figure 5, few-shot gating adaptation enables the detector to dynamically
reweight deception-related directions in a domain-specific manner, leading to more stable and
interpretable score trajectories across different stages of deceptive behavior. In particular, the detector
localizes deceptive spans more precisely, often assigns elevated scores prior to the first explicit
deceptive statement, and appropriately reduces scores once the model transitions back to honest
behavior. These dynamics suggest that adaptive detectors can capture early intent signals and temporal
structure in a way that static, domain-agnostic directions cannot.
7 Conclusion
In conclusion, this paper introduces a novel approach to detecting deception in LLMs by leveraging
Dynamic Deception Subspace Navigation (DDSN). By moving beyond static probing methods
and incorporating iterative orthogonal decomposition and domain-adaptive gating, the proposed
method effectively captures diverse deceptive behaviors across various datasets. Experimental results
demonstrate the superiority of DDSN, achieving high accuracy with minimal adaptation data. This
work provides a valuable step towards more reliable and robust detection mechanisms for LLMs,
enhancing model safety and interpretability before deployment in the real-world applications.
