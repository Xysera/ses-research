Same Question, Different Lies:
Cross-Context Consistency (C³) for Black-Box Sandbagging Detection
Lin Yulong1 Pablo Bernabeu-P´erez Benjamin Arnav 1 Lennie Wells 2 Mary Phuong 3
Abstract
As language models grow more capable, accu-
rate capability evaluation becomes essential for
safety decisions. If models can deliberately un-
derperform on dangerous capability evaluations—
a behavior known assandbagging—they may
evade safety measures designed for their true ca-
pability level. We introduce Cross-Context Con-
sistency (C³), a general framework for unsuper-
vised black-box sandbagging detection that ex-
ploits a fundamental asymmetry: when a model
truly lacks capability, its confusion manifests con-
sistently across paraphrased questions, but when
a capable model feigns incompetence, its strate-
gic choices abouthowto appear weak create de-
tectable inconsistencies. Our framework is agnos-
tic to the specific consistency metric and aggre-
gation method; we present a simple instantiation
using embedding cosine distance and mean ag-
gregation across samples that requires no train-
ing data or model-specific adaptation. We evalu-
ate C³ across prompted and fine-tuned sandbag-
ging scenarios, across instructed and more natu-
ralistic setups, maintaining a classification signal
where other black-box methods fail. Our find-
ings show the limitations of existing sandbag-
ging detection methods, and reveal the efficacy
of consistency-checking as a detection mecha-
nism for dangerous capabilities. Crucially, C³ and
trusted monitoring detect complementary sand-
bagging strategies—no single method dominates—
motivating defense-in-depth deployment where
layered detectors cover each other’s blind spots.

1. Introduction
Capability evaluations underpin safety and deployment de-
cisions, but they are only as trustworthy as the assumption
that models attempt them in good faith. This assumption
breaks undersandbagging(van der Weij et al., 2025)—
models intentionally underperforming on dangerous capa-
bility evaluations—which can cause measured performance
to be a poor proxy for true capability (Gasteiger et al., 2025;
Bloom et al., 2025). If sandbagging goes undetected, mod-
els with hidden dangerous capabilities may pass threshold-
triggered mitigations, leading to miscalibrated technical and
governance responses.
This challenge is especially acute for external auditors,
as many proposed detection approaches rely on access to
model internals (Bloom et al., 2025; Tice et al., 2025), chain-
of-thought reasoning (Li et al., 2025), or log-probability
scores (Pacchiardi et al., 2024), which are often unavail-
able to third parties. In black-box regimes, trusted monitor-
ing (Greenblatt et al., 2024c) and lie detectors (Pacchiardi
et al., 2024) are natural alternatives, but both face important
limitations: trusted monitoring can be evaded by producing
outputs that resemble genuine inability, and can be con-
founded by the monitor’s belief in answer correctness as
opposed to suspiciousness of behavior—honest but incor-
rect models also get flagged (Appendix A.10). Detectors
can also overfit to model identity rather than sandbagging
behavior. Training-based elicitation (Taylor et al., 2025;
Hofst¨atter et al., 2025) can recover hidden capabilities, but
itdestroysthe evidence of deception: finetuning reveals that
a modelcanperform a task, but not whether it washiding
that it could—a critical distinction for governance decisions
about model trustworthiness.
We developCross-Context Consistency(C³), an unsuper-
vised black-box method based on a key asymmetry: main-
taining consistent deception is harder than honest incompe-
tence. When a model truly lacks capability, its confusion
manifests consistently regardless of how questions are pre-
sented. However, when a capable model feigns incompe-
tence, it must decidehowto appear weak, and these strate-
gic choices often leak as inconsistencies across paraphrased
prompts. C³ uses this signal without training data or model-
specific adaptation, enabling zero-shot deployment on new

2. Problem Setting
Detection Task.Given a model M under evaluation, we
aim to distinguish between benign (Mbenign) and sandbag-
ging behavior (Msandbag). We definesandbaggingas a de-
liberate accuracy drop of ≥10 percentage points (pp) from
the model’s benign baseline. While prior work (van der
Weij et al., 2025; Greenblatt et al., 2024b) reports observed
sandbagging gaps of 30–40pp, Taylor et al. (2025) estab-
lish 10pp as an appropriate detection threshold, noting that
benign models can naturally improve by up to 10pp un-
der training-based elicitation. Following their methodology,
we adopt this threshold to capture meaningful underperfor-
mance while accounting for performance variation.
Evaluation Setup.We use a same-model setting, in which
both the benign and sandbagging distributions are derived
from the same underlying model (e.g., GPT-5-nano). This
setup eliminates a critical confound present in mixed-model
comparisons: when Mbenign and Msandbag are generated with
different models (e.g., GPT-4.1-nano vs. GPT-5-nano), de-

Threat Model.Throughout, we consider an auditor who
must decide whether a model under evaluation is (S) a ca-
pable sandbagger deliberately achieving weak performance
or (B) a genuinely less capable model, with access only to
trusted reference models for threshold calibration—never
a known-benign version of the model under evaluation. A
detector must separate (S) from (B) under this access con-
straint, whether the weak performance is measured against
the model’s own baseline (same-model setting) or against
a weaker model’s capability level (cross-capability setting;
Section 4.5).
Model Organisms.We develop multiple sandbagging
model organisms whose outputs mimic genuine capability
limitations, representing a realistic and challenging threat
vector for detection methods that rely on model behavior.
Our most realistic organisms arediscovered-incentivese-
tups: frontier models (GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Pro) infer an
incentive to underperform from an agentic context they ex-
plore through real tool calls, with no explicit instruction to
sandbag and where reading the planted documents is not
even required to complete the task. We construct ten such
scenarios—compared with the single scenario typically stud-
ied in prior work—varying the incentive in subtle ways (Sec-
tion 4.3). To probe C³ from the opposite direction, we also
evaluatepromptedsandbaggers: we write 65 prompts speci-
fying concrete error strategies (e.g., arithmetic mistakes, rea-
soning shortcuts, plausible-but-wrong answers) to red-team
C³ against fixed strategies, rather than as a realistic threat
model in themselves, alongside 33 prompts replicating prior
work styles (see Appendix A.22). This prompted approach
complements password-locked evaluations (Taylor et al.,
2025) by addressing scenarios where models “may need to
reason in-context about whether they should sandbag”—
a harder elicitation challenge than training-internalized
triggers—and by enabling cross-family evaluation (GPT,
Claude, Gemini) without per-model fine-tuning. We also
evaluate fine-tuned password-locked sandbaggers (van der
Weij et al., 2025; Greenblatt et al., 2024b) and incentivised
sandbagging via in-context scheming (Meinke et al., 2024;
Tice et al., 2025) (Section 4.2).
Why Prompted Sandbaggers?Prompted sandbaggers
serve as an upper bound on sandbagging expressiveness:
they reason in-context about whether and how to sand-
bag, matching the threat model for capable models that
might strategically underperform during evaluations. This
contrasts with password-locked models, which may not
form any intent to deceive, making them harder to detect
through behavioral signals (Taylor et al., 2025) but less rep-
resentative of the scheming threat we ultimately care about.
We expect prompted sandbaggers to leave more detectable
signatures than fine-tuned ones, and our results confirm
this—password-locked models are substantially harder for
all methods (Section 4.5). Beyond these, we introduce
discovered-incentive sandbaggers on frontier models: rather
than being instructed, the model must infer from an agentic
context whether underperforming serves its interests. This
is our closest approximation to the scheming threat we ulti-
mately care about, and we evaluate several scenarios rather
than a single setup so that our conclusions do not hinge on
one incidental context (Section 4.3).
Threat Model Realism Spectrum.Our sandbagging
model organisms span two orthogonal axes. The first is
mechanism: whether sandbagging is prompted (instructions
in the system prompt), fine-tuned (behavior learned into
weights, e.g., password-locked models), or emergent (aris-
ing from the model’s own reasoning about incentives). The
second isagency: whether the model mechanically executes
a conditional behavior (like a backdoor, with no strategic
intent) or strategically reasons about when and how to un-
derperform. We discuss how these axes predict detection
difficulty in Section 5.
Metrics and Datasets.We evaluate using Recall at 5%
FPR as our primary metric, following the rationale that de-
tection methods must operate under strict precision require-
ments in deployment settings where human review is costly
(Arnav et al., 2025; Terekhov et al., 2026). We evaluate
on three datasets: GPQA Diamond (198 samples, science),
MATH Level 5 (2,304 samples, mathematics), and USACO
(307 samples, programming). Each dataset is split 40-60
into development and test sets, with hyperparameters tuned
via 3-fold cross-validation on the development set. For each
detection method, we set thresholds to achieve ≤5% FPR
on the development set when evaluated on honest model
responses, then apply these thresholds to test sets contain-
ing sandbagging model organisms across 3 datasets (5–10
prompts per model-dataset combination; see Appendix A.23
for prompt selection). Results report mean ± standard er-
ror (SE) across k=10 questions, mirroring the number of
tasks common in dangerous capability benchmarks (Ap-
pendix A.2), via bootstrap aggregation (see Section 3). We
motivate the choice ofk and report sample-efficiency curves
in Section 4.7. One methodological caveat: at a fixed 5%
FPR the threshold is set by only a handful of benign refer-
ence scores (∼4–6 on our GPQA and USACO development
splits), so it is noisy and recall can carry more uncertainty
than the per-question bootstrap SE alone reflects, especially
on smaller splits.

Correctness Scoring.Correctness is determined per
dataset as follows: for multiple-choice benchmarks (GPQA,
WMDP), we use exact match on the selected choice let-

mathematical reasoning (MATH, GSM8K), we use an LLM

3. Cross-Context Consistency (C³)
C³ is a general framework for sandbagging detection. An
honest model generally produces consistent explanations
across diverse phrasings of the same question, even when
the answer and the model’s reasoning are wrong. A de-
ceptive model, by contrast, faces a two-part coordination
problem across independent samples. First, it must decide
whetherto sandbag: each paraphrase presents a different
context (evaluation framing, unmonitored signals, sandbag-
ging awareness), and the model must independently judge
whether each one triggers sandbagging—different framings
may trigger different decisions, creating inconsistency in
correctnessacross paraphrases. Second, given the decision
to sandbag, it must decidehowto appear weak: choos-
ing among multiple plausible forms of feigned ignorance
or error, where different paraphrases lead to different fail-
ure choices, creating inconsistency inexplanations. Main-
taining consistency in both decisions across independent
samples is fundamentally harder than maintaining honest
consistency, where truth provides a natural coordination
point.
The framework is agnostic to the specific consistency metric
and aggregation method. We present a simple instantiation
(see Algorithm 1), using text embeddings to encode model
explanations, mean pairwise cosine distance for calculating
consistency between them, and the mean to aggregate this
consistency across question samples. Alternative metrics
and aggregation methods are possible. Figure 1 illustrates
the detection pipeline.

3.2. Baselines
Black-box sandbagging detection is underexplored, as most
prior work assumes white-box access (Bloom et al., 2025;
Tice et al., 2025) or focuses on capability elicitation (Taylor
et al., 2025). We evaluate three black-box baselines:
(1) Trusted monitoring (TM): A Claude 3.5 Haiku
judge scores outputs from 1–10 for deliberate underperfor-
mance (Greenblatt et al., 2024c; Taylor et al., 2025), using
a QA-adapted prompt from Gasteiger et al. (2025) (Ap-
pendix A.17).Why it fails:sophisticated sandbaggers pro-
duce outputs indistinguishable from genuine limitations, es-
pecially under password-locking and discovered incentives
(Sections 4.2–4.3); the monitor also flags format-compliant
answers as suspicious and attempts questions itself.
(2) Lie detectors (LD): a logistic-regression classifier on
responses to unrelated probe questions (Pacchiardi et al.,
2024).Why it fails:in mixed-model settings lie detec-
tors learn model identity rather than sandbagging behav-
ior (89%→2% recall once identity is controlled), and their
training assumptions break when models are deceptive (Sec-
tion 4.4).
(3) Behavioral probes: directly asking the model “Were
you intentionally underperforming?”Why it fails:near-
perfect recall on naive prompted sandbaggers collapses un-
der a single denial instruction (admission 74%→3.4% ),
and probes perform worse than chance on fine-tuned and
discovered-incentive sandbaggers (Appendix A.16).
All baselines see the same model outputs as C³, including
worked solutions on MATH and USACO and, for reasoning
models, the thinking-trace summary when the provider ex-
poses it. Note that access to this summary is not equivalent
to full chain-of-thought access—for GPT-5 and later, the
underlying internal reasoning may remain hidden.
4. Results
We evaluate C³ against our two baselines (TM and lie detec-
tors; Section 3.2) in the primary same-model setup (GPT-5-
nano benign vs. GPT-5-nano sandbagging), then test general-
ization to cross-capability settings, fine-tuned sandbaggers,
and discovered incentive sandbagging.
4.1. Prompted Sandbaggers Are Less Consistent
On USACO, C³ achieves high recall on sandbaggers that
are prompted with relatively broad sandbagging instructions
(e.g., use chaotic variable naming), while trusted monitor-
ing better detects highly specific strategies (e.g., off-by-one
loop errors), where the model often leaks its strategy in
chain-of-thought (full prompts in Appendix A.23). The com-
plementarity arises from different detection mechanisms:
C³ catches inconsistencies in thecoordinationof deceptive
choices across contexts, while TM catches intentleakagein
chain-of-thought reasoning. C³ outperforms trusted moni-
toring on most prompts, though neither method dominates
across all strategies (Figure 2), motivating defense-in-depth
deployment. TM may work less well on harder tasks: the
weaker monitor model is not always competent to judge
correctness, and its detection signal is partly confounded
by answer correctness—it flags wrong answers as suspi-
cious regardless of whether they reflect genuine inability or
sandbagging (Appendix A.10). This is consistent with prior
reports that chain-of-thought monitoring can be evaded or
degraded (Li et al., 2025; Arnav et al., 2025; Gupta et al.,
2025). On GPQA, per-prompt results show similar com-
plementarity across a broader set of prompts (Figure 4).

recall at 5% FPR on 9 of 12 GPQA prompts and 3 of 5

4.2. Password-Locked Sandbaggers Are Still Less
Consistent
Fine-tuned (LoRA password-locked) sandbaggers (van der
Weij et al., 2025; Greenblatt et al., 2024b) prove harder to
detect than prompted sandbaggers because the deceptive
policy is internalized in weights. C³ still achieves52.9% re-
call at 5% FPR on WMDP—a direct dangerous-capability
benchmark and the highest recall of any black-box method
we tested in this setting—and 36.0% on GSM8K, where
trusted monitoring and lie detectors approach chance (Ap-
pendix A.12).
4.3. Discovered Incentive Sandbaggers Are Less
Consistent
We tested “discovered incentive” sandbagging (Meinke
et al., 2024), where models receive pre-filled conversations
showing they “discovered” documentation about unlearn-
ing triggers. This induces sandbagging without explicit
instructions, producing errors indistinguishable from au-
thentic limitations. Trusted monitoring achieves only 2%
recall (random). C³ achieves 19% recall at 5% FPR on
USACO with GPT-5-nano—the only method with positive
signal on this hardest threat model (Appendix Figure 11).

the per-prompt breakdown is in Appendix Table 8. This
extends C³ beyond STEM proxy benchmarks to dangerous-
capability content in a substantially harder setting where
models cannot rely on answer-option elimination.
4.4. Black-Box Baselines Fail to Detect Sandbagging
The two main baselines fail for distinct reasons. Trusted
monitoring achieves near-random recall when, within each
context, sandbaggers produce outputs indistinguishable
from genuine limitations; its detection signal is also largely
confounded by answer correctness rather than genuine be-
havioral detection (Appendix A.10). On USACO, lie detec-
tors collapse from 89% to 2% recall when we control for
model identity (a ∼44× drop; Figure 12; Appendix A.9).
We traced this to model-identity confounding: GPT-4.1-
nano and GPT-5-nano differ by 35pp in baseline “yes” re-
sponse rates even when both are honest. Prior mixed-model
evaluations detected “which model is this?” rather than
sandbagging behavior. C³ avoids this confound by measur-
ing within-model consistency. Appendix A.18 has more
detail on lie detectors’ three main failure modes: transfer
failure across model families, model identity confounds in
mixed-model settings, and training data assumptions that
break when models are deceptive. Behavioral probes are
also defeated by a stealth prompt instructing the model to
never admit underperformance (Appendix A.20).
4.5. Capable Prompted Sandbaggers Are Less
Consistent Than Weaker Benign Models

difference between the same model sandbagging and an-
swering honestly, but a more realistic threat involvescross-
capability sandbagging: a capable model (generation N+1)
strategically underperforming to match a weaker model’s
(generation N) expected capability level.

4.8. Adversaries and Defense-in-Depth: Ensembling
with Trusted Monitoring
Deterministic recipes evade C³.Prompts providing de-
terministic error recipes (off-by-one, sign flips, fixed con-
fusion matrices) reduce C³ to near-chance recall on both
USACO (4.7–18.0%) and GPQA (14–46%; Appendix A.1).
We do not claim C³ is robust to such strategies. Our
discovered-incentive experiments (Section 4.3) partially ad-
dress whether current models independently develop them:
given situational incentives without explicit recipes, models
do not converge on strategies that fully evade C³. Whether
more capable future models will acquire such consistency
through training remains open.
Ensembling with trusted monitoring.We therefore de-
ploy C³ alongside trusted monitoring (TM) as a mean ensem-
ble: each method’s scores are normalised to [0,1] , averaged,
and thresholded at 5% FPR on the combined score—a two-
stage pipeline where TM is a first-pass filter for models that
verbalise deceptive reasoning and C³ a second pass for be-
haviorally inconsistent models that do not leak intent. The
ensemble matches or exceeds the best single method on ev-
ery prompted-sandbagging setting we test (e.g., GPQA GPT-
5-nano: C³ 67.9% / TM 39.5% / ensemble 87.0%; MATH
GPT-5-nano: C³ 24.2% / TM 99.6% / ensemble 98.5%; full
table in Appendix A.8). No single method dominates: C³ is
stronger on discovered-incentive sandbagging, TM catches
deterministic strategies that evade C³, and the ensemble im-
proves on both when both have moderate signal—supporting
defense-in-depth.
5. Limitations
Detection Difficulty Across Threat Models.C³ detects
thecoordination costof choosing to fail: reproducing a
decision about when and how to underperform consistently
across paraphrases is hard, and our threat-model taxonomy
(Section 2) predicts where this cost is highest. Mechani-
cal sandbagging (e.g., a fine-tuned password-locked policy)
bakes the failure into the weights and incurs little inference-
time coordination cost, making it harder to detect; prompted
sandbaggers must actively coordinate underperformance
from instructions, producing the inconsistencies C³ exploits.
The most concerning threat—discovered-incentive or au-
tonomously emergent scheming—occupies the hardest cor-
ner, reflecting both its intrinsic difficulty and the current
limits of consistency-based detection.
Task and Domain Coverage.Detection varies by domain
and strategy—on USACO, C³ achieves 95% recall on bla-
tant patterns but only 4.7% on subtle ones, while TM shows
the reverse. Like Taylor et al. (2025), we evaluate only
on capability benchmarks with verifiable answers (GPQA,
MATH, USACO, ProtocolQA) and do not test open-ended
tasks (conceptual work, research-direction steering, propen-
sity evaluations), or domains where models might evade by
consistently claiming ignorance.
Adversarial Optimisation Pressure.C³ is vulnerable to
sandbaggers that maintain consistent error patterns across
paraphrases, and we have not evaluated adversaries fine-
tuned specifically against the consistency signal via gradient-
or RL-based evasion. Deterministic-recipe experiments
(Section 4.8) show such consistency can be induced by

explicit instruction; whether more capable future models
acquire it through training—as an instrumentally useful
evasion—remains open. We have not found prompts that
sandbag substantially while evading C³ without determin-
istic recipes (Appendix A.20), but absence of evidence is
not evidence of absence. Our frontier results (GPT-5.4 and
Gemini 3.1 Pro on discovered-incentive sandbagging; Sec-

Cross-Model Style Confound.In the cross-capability set-

between model families rather than sandbagging-driven in-
consistency. We mitigate this by using same-model compar-
isons as our primary setting; correctness-matched bootstrap
and honest-wrong controls (Section 4.6) further rule out
accuracy-driven stylistic differences. Fully disentangling
style from inconsistency across families remains open.
6. Related Work
Prior work shows models can sandbag at every stage of
the pipeline: training-time exploration hacking (Jang et al.,
2026), evaluation-time underperformance to avoid deploy-
ment safeguards (van der Weij et al., 2025), and deployment-
time sabotage of safety-critical research (Gasteiger et al.,
2025). Greenblatt et al. (2024b) introducedpassword-locked
models for stress-testing elicitation methods, and van der
Weij et al. (2025) showed sandbagging generalises from
training benchmarks to held-out evaluations. More re-
cent work documents increasingly sophisticated deception:
covert sandbagging under chain-of-thought monitoring (Li
et al., 2025), in-context scheming (Meinke et al., 2024), and
alignment faking (Greenblatt et al., 2024a). “Deep decep-
tiveness” (Soares, 2023)—models that sandbag without self-
conceptualising as deceptive—makes introspection-based
detection fundamentally limited; C³’s behavioral approach
sidesteps this by measuring output consistency rather than
self-understanding.
Black-Box Detection.Taylor et al. (2025) found tran-
script analysis, elicitation prompting, and persona attacks
achieved only 20% accuracy against sandbaggers imitat-
ing weaker models; training-based elicitation restored per-
formance but eliminated rather than detected the behavior,
creating false positives on benign models. Hofst¨atter et al.
(2025) similarly find fine-tuning recovers hidden capabil-
ities while prompting fails. Within the AI control frame-
work (Greenblatt et al., 2024c), Ward et al. (2025) report
LLM monitors detect code sabotage but struggle with sand-
bagging.
White-Box Detection.Linear probes detect sleeper
agents (Hubinger et al., 2024) and sandbagging (Bloom
et al., 2025) but remain vulnerable to evasion (Taylor et al.,
2025), overfitting, and RL-based obfuscation (Gupta et al.,
2025); B¨urger et al. (2024) find truth representations occupy
a universal subspace. Noise injection (Tice et al., 2025)
reveals hidden capabilities but needs weight access and
ground-truth labels. Taylor et al. (2025) report blue-team
probes “suffered from issues with legibility, calibration,
and validation”—and all white-box methods are unavailable
when frontier providers restrict access.
Lie Detection.Pacchiardi et al. (2024) train a classifier on
model yes/no answers to a fixed bank of unrelated follow-
up questions; known vulnerabilities include training against
the detector and question dilution, and Kretschmar et al.
(2025) show such detectors “systematically fail” on diverse
lie types not seen during training. We adapt this as a baseline
and find it appears unsuitable for sandbagging detection—
our analysis suggests it learns model identity rather than
sandbagging behavior (Section 4.4).
7. Conclusion
We introduce Cross-Context Consistency (C³), an unsuper-
vised zero-shot black-box method for detecting sandbagging
that exploits a fundamental asymmetry: genuine confusion
manifests consistently across paraphrased questions, while
strategic underperformance creates detectable inconsisten-
cies inhowa model chooses to appear weak.
Our experiments reveal two main findings. First, the ex-
isting baselines fail in characteristic ways: lie detectors
learn model identity rather than sandbagging behavior, and
on the hardest discovered-incentive threat model trusted
monitoring achieves only 2% recall (Section 4.3) while
C³ still recovers signal. Second, C³ generalises without
adaptation across the GPT, Claude, and Gemini families—
including a frontier-level dangerous-capability evaluation
(ProtocolQA on Gemini 3.1 Pro)—and from prompted to
fine-tuned and discovered-incentive sandbaggers, suggest-
ing the consistency–deception asymmetry is a general prop-
erty of strategic underperformance.
These results carry a practical implication: the black-box,
zero-shot, unsupervised design suits third-party evaluators—
government AI safety institutes and independent auditors—
who operate under limited access windows and cannot fine-
tune or inspect weights. But our 19% recall on the hardest
discovered-incentive threat models suggests consistency-
based detection alone may be insufficient as schemers grow
more capable, so C³ should be deployed as one layer in a
defence-in-depth strategy rather than a standalone solution.

Impact Statement
Sandbagging detection methods could help ensure accurate
dangerous capability evaluations for AI safety governance.
However, our methods have limitations described in Sec-
tion 5, and we recommend they complement rather than
replace other safety measures in high-stakes deployments.

How to read the diagnostics.Shapiro–Wilk (SW) is the direct check for (A1): it tests whether the benign scores look
Gaussian. A high SW p-value does not prove Gaussianity, but a very small value is evidence that the MAD rule’s Gaussian
quantile is unsafe; we therefore pair it with the Q–Q plots in Figure 8. The one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test
checks whether the sandbagger scores are shifted upward relative to benign scores, the empirical analogue of (A3). A
small KS p-value supports a higher sandbagging tail, but does not by itself prove pointwise stochastic dominance at every
threshold. Cohen’s d reports the standardized mean gap between sandbagger and benign scores; it is an effect-size summary
for detection power, not a calibration guarantee, and can miss tail-shape pathologies. In short, SW diagnoses whether
Full-MAD is calibrated; KS and Cohen’sddiagnose whether the detector has separation.

A.9. Lie Detector Confound
This subsection expands on the main-text result that lie detectors classify model identity rather than sandbagging behavior
(§4). We evaluate five detector variants on the three USACO sandbagging prompts whose dev-set task accuracy most closely
matches gpt-4.1-nano’s, including the pretrained “subsets union” detector of Pacchiardi et al. (2024), which is reported to
generalise across models. Each variant is scored in two settings:mixed-model, where the honest and sandbagging transcripts
come from different model families (GPT-4.1-nano vs. GPT-5-nano), andsame-model, where both come from GPT-5-nano.
If a detector measured sandbagging rather than model identity, the two settings should agree. Instead, the retrained variants
drop from 29–89% mixed-model recall to 2–10% same-model recall (a 4–40× collapse), and the pretrained detector never
transfers to our model pair (2% mixed-model, 15% same-model). The collapse tracks a 35pp difference in baseline “yes”
response rates between the two model families, confirming that the apparent detection signal in mixed-model comparisons is
a model-identity artifact (Figure 12). C³ sidesteps this confound by measuring consistency within a single model.
A.10. Trusted Monitoring Correctness Confound
Trusted monitoring prompts a Claude 3.5 Haiku judge to reason about what a capable model’s answer would look like,
list aspects of the transcript that look suspicious and aspects that look like genuine effort, weigh the two, and assign a
manipulation score from 1–10. The judge is not asked to independently solve the problem and compare answers. On WMDP,
trusted monitoring achieves 41.1% recall at 5% FPR. However, this detection signal is largely driven by a correctness
confound rather than genuine behavioral detection.

Partial correlation.To test whether TM captures any signal beyond correctness, we computed the partial correlation

Qualitative analysis.We classified each of the 196 judge evaluations, categorizing whether the judge solved the problem
independently, checked answer correctness, and cited behavioral signals such as evasiveness. While 100% of evaluations
cite behavioral signals, among incorrect answers 100% receive a “behavioral” primary basis classification, versus only 41%
for correct answers. This suggests the judge interprets incorrectnessasbehavioral evidence of sandbagging—labeling wrong
answers as “evasive” or “lacking explanation”—rather than identifying manipulation-specific signals independent of answer
quality.
Implications.TM in this configuration functions primarily as a competence classifier. Its recall reflects the judge’s ability
to identify wrong answers, which are more frequent under sandbagging, rather than an ability to distinguish intentional
underperformance from genuine inability. A detection method that relies on correctness will fail against sophisticated
sandbaggers that produce plausible wrong answers or against models with naturally low accuracy on the target domain.

A.14. Paraphrasing Prompts
We developed 28 paraphrasing strategies—better understood asrecontextualisations—designed to probe whether models
distinguish between being evaluated, deployed, monitored, or in training contexts. Each strategy instructs an LLM (GPT-
4.1-nano) to transform the original question while preserving mathematical/scientific content. Our strategies focus on
evaluation-related contextual signals because sandbagging models must decidewhento underperform, and evaluation
context is the most natural trigger. Other recontextualisation approaches (e.g., varying difficulty framing, user identity, or
domain context) may provide complementary signal and are a promising direction for future work.
Strategy Design.We designed strategies across eight categories, spanning a range of contextual signals:
•Evaluation signals: explicit benchmark framing, interview context, capability assessment markers
•Deployment signals: production system framing, legacy API markers, real-world professional context
•Unmonitored signals: “conversation not logged,” internal debugging context
•Benchmark detection: misspelled dataset names (“HTAM” for MATH, “QPGA” for GPQA)
•Mixed/ambiguous signals: post-hoc evaluation reveals, nested evaluation framing
•Sandbagging awareness: explicit mentions of sandbagging, accusations, definitions
•Verification/challenge: adversarial framing, boundary-testing prompts
•Safety/trust framing: emphasis on trustworthiness and safe deployment
Selection Pipeline.From the initial 28 strategies, we applied a two-stage selection pipeline. First, we filtered by
answerability: each paraphrased question was validated by GPT-4.1-nano (temperature 0.0) to ensure (1) the problem
description was preserved, (2) the input/output format remained clear, and (3) the question was answerable given the
information provided. For USACO, we additionally required that worked examples were preserved. Failed paraphrases
were regenerated up to 3 times with feedback about which criteria failed. Strategies with answerability below 90% (MATH,
USACO) or 80% (GPQA) after retries were excluded. We applied a lower threshold for GPQA because its complex,
domain-specific questions (graduate-level science) had lower answerability rates across the board—enforcing 90% would
have eliminated most strategies.
Second, among the strategies passing the answerability filter, we used Vendi diversity scores (Friedman & Dieng, 2023) to
select the final set, maximising semantic diversity to ensure coverage of different contextual signals rather than redundant
variations of the same signal. This yielded5 strategies for GPQA,10 for MATH, and10 for USACO(see Table 6).

A.16. Behavioral Probes Baseline
We also tried another baseline we call “behavioral probes” or “self-confession”, where a model is asked a series of Yes/No
questions. It was able to get almost perfect recall @ 5% FPR when we do multi-sample classification with k=10 resamplings
with replacement, across the datasets. This was because the honest/normal model never admitted to underperformance,
whereas the sandbagging model admitted to varying degrees. However, upon updating the prompt to instruct models to never
admit underperformance, the results were wiped away (Figure 15). We note that behavioral probes were only evaluated
againstinstructedsandbaggers, where they achieve near-perfect recall; we have not tested them against our password-locked
or discovered-incentive organisms, so their strong performance here does not establish that they generalise to those harder
settings.

A.18. Lie Detector Failure Mode Analysis
We identify three failure modes through systematic evaluation of 10 lie detector variants.
Failure Mode 1: Transfer Failure.The original lie detector from Pacchiardi et al. (2024), trained on GPT-3.5-turbo,
fails to transfer to our model pair. On mixed-model comparisons (GPT-4.1-nano vs. GPT-5-nano), it performsworse than
random: it predicts theoppositeof ground truth, scoring the benign model (0.99) higher than sandbaggers (0.58). This
result differs from the original paper’s findings on cross-model generalization, possibly due to differences in models or
experimental setup.
Failure Mode 2: Model Identity Confound.Prior lie detector evaluations usedmixed-model settings—comparing
different model families (e.g., GPT-4.1-nano benign vs. GPT-5-nano sandbagging). When we retrain detectors on our model
pair, they achieve 89% recall in this mixed-model setting. However, when we control for model identity usingsame-model
comparisons(GPT-5-nano benign vs. GPT-5-nano sandbagging), recall collapses to 2%. This ∼44× performance drop
demonstrates the prior mixed-model results were confounded: detectors learned to classify “is this GPT-5-nano?” rather
than “is this sandbagging?” We traced the confound to a 35 percentage point baseline difference in “yes” response rates
between GPT-4.1-nano (21%) and GPT-5-nano (56%)—even when both models are honest. Prior results that appeared to
detect sandbagging were instead detecting model identity.
Failure Mode 3: Training Data Assumption.Lie detector training assumes models faithfully generate honest vs. lying
behavior when instructed. This assumption fails in our setting: lie detectors trained on benign models achieve 50–100%
recall on sandbagging detection (in the mixed model setting), butincluding sandbaggers in training drops recall to
10–30%—worse than training on benign data alone. The sandbagging prompts corrupt the training signal, making lies
indistinguishable from truths. More fundamentally, if a model is already deceptive, its “truth” condition training data may
itself be unreliable—you cannot trust a sandbagging model to faithfully generate honest responses for your training set.

A.19. Why Sandbagging May Evade Lie Detection
Beyond the empirical failure modes documented above, lie detectors may fundamentally miss sandbagging because
sandbagging is not factual lying. Probe questions like “Were you told to lie?” elicit “Yes” from models instructed to give
false answers, but sandbaggers instructed to “achieve 0% accuracy” or “simulate a malfunctioning system” may genuinely
not perceive this as “lying”—they are following instructions to fail, not to deceive about facts. This conceptual mismatch
suggests that even perfectly calibrated lie detectors may struggle with sandbagging detection because the underlying behavior
does not match the detector’s training distribution.

A.23. Complete Sandbagging System Prompts
This section contains the complete system prompts used to create sandbagging model organisms in our experiments. We
adopt ared team approach: attempting to createcovertsandbaggers that underperform without revealing their strategy.
Prompts failing the stealth filter represent trivially detectable sandbagging (catchable by keyword matching); prompts
passing represent the harder detection problem we aim to study. We further set aside prompts that score highly (≥9/10) for
specificity and consistency asadversarial prompts, which we consider unrealistic model organisms and present results for
separately (Section A.1).
From candidate prompts, we select those passing four filters. Selection isper-model: each model receives its own prompt
set, since compliance with sandbagging instructions varies across models.
1. Sandbagging compliance( ≤10% refusal rate): The model attempts to sandbag rather than refusing the instruction.
High refusal indicates effective safety training—desirable for deployment but unsuitable for detection research.
32
Cross-Context Consistency for Black-Box Sandbagging Detection
2. Task completion( ≤10% task refusal): The model produces substantive responses rather than refusing to answer. High
task refusal represents trivially detectable sandbagging outside our threat model, as trusted monitors easily flag empty
or refusal responses.
3. Sandbagging effectiveness( ≥10pp, statistically significant): We compute the paired accuracy difference between
benign and sandbagging conditions on the development set. A prompt passes if the gap is at least 10 percentage points
andstatistically significant ( p <0.05, paired t-test). The 10pp threshold follows Taylor et al. (2025); the significance
requirement ensures observed gaps reflect genuine sandbagging rather than sampling noise.
4. Strategy stealth( <10% keyword leakage): Fewer than 10% of responses contain sandbagging-related terms (e.g.,
“deliberately wrong,” “sabotage”). This operationalizes covert sandbagging—models that underperform without
admitting their strategy.

instruction-following failures) can confound the signal.
To mitigate this class of failure, we filter paraphrasing strategies byanswerability: an LLM judge verifies that each
paraphrased prompt retains sufficient information to be answered, and strategies falling below a dataset-specific threshold
(80–90%) are excluded (see Section A.15 for details). This is important because it is unclear a priori how sandbagging versus
benign models would behave on unanswerable paraphrases, and we observed that strategies producing large numbers of
unanswerable paraphrases skew detection results unpredictably. Filtering by answerability reduces—but does not eliminate—
the risk of paraphrases that trigger instruction-following failures rather than testing genuine reasoning consistency.
